Understanding the Overlap Between Anxiety and Depression

If you feel worried and exhausted at the same time, or if you cannot tell whether you are anxious, depressed, or both, you are not imagining things. The anxiety and depression overlap is one of the most common experiences people bring into therapy, and it can be confusing to live with.

What This Experience Can Look Like in Real Life

The overlap between anxiety and depression does not always look dramatic. It often looks like functioning on the outside while feeling worn down and unsettled on the inside.

Anxiety and depression are often talked about as separate conditions, but in real life they frequently show up together. You might feel restless and on edge one moment, then flat and unmotivated the next. This mix can make it harder to name what you are feeling, which can make it harder to ask for the right kind of support.

Example 1:
You wake up already anticipating everything that could go wrong that day, but you also feel too heavy to get out of bed. By the time you are moving, you feel behind and defeated before the day has even started.

Example 2:
You push through work and responsibilities, staying busy so you do not have to sit with how you feel. At night, your mind will not slow down, even though you are running on empty.

Example 3:
You used to enjoy things that now feel like one more task. Underneath the low mood, there is also a steady hum of worry about falling further behind.

Many people describe this as feeling stuck between two states, wired and worn out at once. That combination is exactly why the anxiety and depression overlap can be so hard to identify on your own.

At Get Real Therapy, many of the people we work with are not dealing with pure anxiety or pure depression. They are dealing with both, layered on top of each other. If this feels familiar, support through anxiety therapy or depression therapy can help you understand what is happening beneath the surface.

Common Signs Include:

  • Worrying constantly while also feeling too tired to do anything about it
  • Racing thoughts paired with low motivation
  • Irritability that hides sadness underneath it
  • Trouble sleeping, either from a busy mind or from low energy
  • Feeling on edge and emotionally numb at the same time
  • Avoiding things out of both dread and exhaustion

Why Anxiety and Depression Often Happen Together

Anxiety and depression share some of the same underlying territory, even though they can look different on the surface. Both involve a nervous system that has been under pressure for a long time.

When worry, overthinking, or hypervigilance continue without much relief, they can eventually lead to exhaustion, hopelessness, and low motivation. That exhaustion is often where depression begins to take hold. At the same time, depression can bring its own layer of dread, self-criticism, and fear about the future, which can feel a lot like anxiety.

Patterns That Can Contribute to the Overlap:

  • Chronic stress that has never fully had a chance to settle
  • Perfectionism or over-responsibility that leaves little room to rest
  • Unresolved trauma or difficult past experiences
  • Avoidance patterns that reduce anxiety in the short term but increase isolation over time
  • A long history of pushing through instead of processing what you feel

None of this means something is wrong with you. It usually means your mind and body have been managing a lot for a long time, and both anxiety and depression are signs of that, not proof that you have failed to cope.

How Therapy Can Help With Anxiety and Depression Together

Therapy does not require you to separate anxiety from depression before you can get support. In fact, treating them together often makes more sense, since they tend to feed into each other.

In Therapy, You May Work Toward:

  • Understanding how anxiety and depression show up specifically for you
  • Identifying the patterns and triggers underneath both
  • Learning how your nervous system responds to stress and low mood
  • Building tools for regulation, rest, and motivation
  • Reducing shame around needing support for more than one thing at once
  • Creating more steadiness in daily life, even on harder days

When It May Be Time to Reach Out

You do not have to wait until things feel unmanageable to ask for support. Therapy can be helpful when:

  • You feel both anxious and low more days than not
  • Your energy and motivation have been declining
  • Worry and heaviness are both affecting your sleep
  • You are pushing through on the outside while struggling underneath
  • Relationships or work are being affected
  • You are not sure what to call what you are feeling, only that it is a lot

Sometimes people wait because they think they need to pick one thing to focus on first. You do not. Support can address anxiety and depression at the same time.

What to Do Next

If you have been living with a mix of worry and low mood, the next step does not have to be complicated. Start by noticing the pattern. Pay attention to whether your mind feels busy, your body feels heavy, or both at once, and how long this has been going on.

If the anxiety and depression overlap sounds like your experience, therapy can help you make sense of it and find a way forward that does not rely on just pushing through.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you have anxiety and depression at the same time?

Yes. It is common for anxiety and depression to occur together, and many people experience symptoms of both at once.

What are signs of anxiety and depression together?

Signs can include constant worry mixed with low motivation, fatigue paired with restlessness, trouble sleeping, irritability, and feeling both on edge and emotionally flat.

Does anxiety cause depression or does depression cause anxiety?

Either can lead to the other. Ongoing anxiety can wear a person down into depression, and depression can create worry and dread that feels like anxiety.

Can therapy treat anxiety and depression together?

Yes. Therapy can address anxiety and depression at the same time by focusing on the shared patterns, thoughts, and nervous system responses underneath both.

How do I know if it is anxiety, depression, or both?

A therapist can help you sort out what you are experiencing. You do not need a clear label before reaching out for support.
